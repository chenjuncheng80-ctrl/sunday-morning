# Sunday Morning — introduction to the adaptation

**Xi Xi, "Sunday Morning", translated by Howard Goldblatt**
*A playable adaptation in the form of a short browser game.*

Xi Xi's "Sunday Morning" is a poem built out of brackets. Nearly every line ends with a set of choices — the weather, the mirror, breakfast, the afternoon — and none of them is more correct than the others. Reading it, I felt the poem was not describing a morning but offering one. So I turned it into something you can walk through: a small, drawn game that takes place in a single room.

The adaptation is a browser game. Nothing to install: you open a link and you are in a bedroom at seven in the morning. Four numbers stay on screen — the time, the weather, your energy and your coins — because these are the things the poem quietly worries about. You decide what time it is, look out of the window, look in the mirror, open the fridge, put on your jeans, and decide what to do with the afternoon. Almost every option is written in the poem's own words: "a pall of loneliness", "a lotus in full bloom", "the teahouse is sure to be packed", "there'll be a fountain here". I did not paraphrase them: I wanted players to click on Xi Xi's language, not on my summary of it.

My intention was to make the poem's humour physical. The poem is funny in a dry way — you lose two coins and the explanation is that bad things happen to Leos — but on the page that joke lasts a second. In a game you have to watch the coins roll under the wardrobe, and then decide whether to move the furniture. You usually don't. The same is true of the empty fridge, and of an afternoon that disappears while you are choosing how to spend it.

The room does most of the storytelling. I drew it in a warm, paper-like style and let the weather change the light indoors, not just outside the window. When a storm arrives, the walls turn grey-blue and the glass runs with water. By three in the afternoon the light is gold and crosses the floor. The clock on the wall keeps the real time, and its hands move as yours do. Nothing in the room is only decoration; everything is a clock.

My goals were specific. First, I wanted a reader who has never met the poem to finish in ten minutes and still feel its shape: a day of small freedoms that add up to something like loneliness. Second, I wanted the ending to stay open. The doorbell rings, six things could be behind the door, and the game lets you choose — the same unresolved gesture the poem ends on. Third, I wanted it to work on a phone, with no instructions and no fail state. There is no way to lose this Sunday; you can only spend it differently.

Making it taught me that adaptation is mostly subtraction. I removed the narrator, most of my own descriptions, and every line that was mine rather than Xi Xi's. What is left is a room, a clock, and a set of brackets waiting to be filled.
